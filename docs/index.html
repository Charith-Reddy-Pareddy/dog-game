Dog Game

A two-player, general-sum game: a red house and a blue house each want a shared "dog" as close as possible to their own doorstep. Each round both sides pick a point to pull toward; the dog lands on the w / (1−w) blend of those two points. Because the domain is bounded, players are pushed to exaggerate toward the edge to counteract each other — the same logic behind two influencers each staking out an exaggerated opinion to drag a shared audience toward them. Source, tests, and training code: github.com/Charith-Reddy-Pareddy/dog-game.

Training results: does policy gradient actually converge?

These numbers come from real self-play training runs (PyTorch REINFORCE, 1500 episodes each, 5 house/weight configurations × 3 network architectures — reproduce with python3 -m doggame.experiments), not from this page. Exploitability here is the same check as the live simulator above, applied to the trained policy's learned actions.

Finding 1 — "can this converge?" needs a number attached to it. 14 of 15 (config, architecture) runs reached exploitability below 0.004 within 1500 episodes. The 15th — separate architecture on the (0.8,0.8)/(0.2,0.3) config — was still at 0.183, clearly unconverged. Training that exact run 3000 episodes further brought it to 0.004: not stuck, just slower on one action dimension. Policy gradient does converge here, but "1500 episodes" isn't a number you can quote without naming the configuration and architecture it was measured on.
Finding 2 — multiple equilibria are real, not a bug. When both houses share a y-coordinate (e.g. (0.95,0.5) and (0.05,0.5)), the y-axis of the game becomes degenerate: any pair of y-actions that averages back to that shared value is equally optimal. The analytical best-response solver lands on the "obvious" solution (both players at y = 0.5); trained policies instead consistently found a boundary-cancellation solution (red near y = 1, blue near y = 0). Both check out as genuinely unexploitable (< 0.0002) — two different, equally valid equilibria, which is exactly the non-uniqueness the source research predicts once houses share a coordinate.
